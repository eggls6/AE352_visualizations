Rigid body dynamics: reference frames and Euler's equations

Introduction

A rigid body moving in space has six degrees of freedom (DoF). Those can be decomposed into three DoF for the position of its center of mass and three DoF for its orientation. This explorer only considers the latter, i.e. rotation. The body is a uniformly dense, solid ellipsoid with semi-axes a, b, c turning about its fixed center of mass.

Describing the rotation involves two frames of reference. The inertial frame (XI, YI, ZI) is fixed in space. The body-fixed frame (xb, yb, zb) is locked to and rotates with the body. Newton's laws retain their simplest form in the inertial frame, but the inertia tensor that provides the "resistance" of a body to acceleration along a given axis is constant only in the body frame. Euler's equations are, therefore, written in rotating body axes and Euler angles are needed to describe how the two frames relate.

Synopsis

  • Orientation. The 3-2-1 Euler angles rotate the inertial frame into the body frame in three steps: yaw ψ about ZI, then pitch θ about the new y axis, then roll φ about xb. The simulation itself integrates a unit quaternion, which never runs into gimbal lock. The Euler angles are computed from it for display.
  • Kinematics. The body angular velocity ω = [p q r]ᵀ is a linear combination of the Euler-angle rates, but it is not equal to them. The matrix shown in the Kinematics tab converts one to the other. Turning the rates back into Euler-angle rates requires dividing by cos θ, so the conversion fails at θ = ±90°.
  • Euler's equations. In the inertial frame, dH/dt = M. In principal body axes this becomes M = I ω̇ + ω × (I ω). The cross-product terms, such as (I₃ − I₂) q r, are gyroscopic couplings. They let the angular velocity change even when no external moment acts.
  • Conservation laws. With M = 0, the angular momentum vector H stays fixed in the inertial frame, and the kinetic energy T = ½ ωᵀIω stays constant. Seen from the body, H keeps its length but changes direction. Its tip moves along a curve where the sphere |H| = const meets the energy ellipsoid.
  • Poinsot's picture. In the body view, the tip of ω traces a closed curve called the polhode. In the inertial view it traces the herpolhode, which lies in a fixed plane perpendicular to H. Both trails are drawn in the views.
  • Stability of spin. Steady spin about the axis with the largest or the smallest moment of inertia is stable. Spin about the intermediate axis is unstable: a tiny wobble grows until the body flips over, then the flip repeats periodically. This is called the tennis-racket or Dzhanibekov effect. With friction or flexing, only spin about the major axis is truly stable. That is not modeled here.

Things to investigate

  • Compare the two views. Without external torques the inertial axes and H remain fixed the inertial frame while the body tumbles. In the body view the ellipsoid is at rest and the inertial axes move.
  • Run the three spin presets. Only the intermediate-axis spin flips.
  • Turn off "Torque-free" and apply a moment. With the body already spinning, a moment about one axis makes the angular velocity swing toward another axis. This is the gyroscopic response.
  • Make the body symmetric by setting b = c. The polhode becomes a circle and the motion becomes steady precession.
  • Raise the integration step Δt and watch ΔT/T₀ grow. RK4 is accurate but does not conserve energy exactly.
