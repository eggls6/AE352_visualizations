The double pendulum

Introduction

The double pendulum is a staple of nonlinear dynamical systems theory. A second pendulum hangs from the bob of the first, both subject to gravity. Two point masses m₁ and m₂ sit on massless rigid rods of length l₁ and l₂, and both swing freely in a vertical plane. There is no friction and no driving force.

The double pendulum is one of the simplest mechanical systems that shows deterministic chaos. The equations are exact and fully deterministic, yet at high enough energy the motion cannot be predicted in practice: two starts that differ by a millionth of a radian soon follow completely different paths.

Synopsis

  • Equations of motion. With the two angles θ₁ and θ₂ (measured from the vertical) as coordinates, Lagrange's equations give the coupled, nonlinear pair shown at the top of the page. The simulation solves them for θ̈₁ and θ̈₂ through numerical integration.
  • Energy conservation. With no friction or forcing, the total energy E is conserved. The motion therefore stays on a three-dimensional energy surface inside the four-dimensional phase space (θ₁, ω₁, θ₂, ω₂). The drift readout in the State panel checks the integrator against this.
  • Small oscillations. Near the hanging state the system behaves like two coupled linear oscillators with two normal modes. For equal masses and lengths the frequencies are ω² = (2 ∓ √2) g/l. In the slower in-phase mode θ₂/θ₁ = +√2; in the faster anti-phase mode θ₂/θ₁ = −√2. The two normal-mode presets start exactly on these modes.
  • Sensitivity to initial conditions. The twin pendulum starts 10⁻⁶ rad away. In the chaotic regime the log-separation plot climbs roughly in a straight line, which means the gap grows exponentially. The slope estimates the largest Lyapunov exponent. In the regular regime the separation only grows slowly, roughly in proportion to time.
  • From order to chaos. At low energy the motion is quasi-periodic. Each phase space trajectory stays on an invariant torus and traces a closed curve in the Poincaré section. As the energy rises, these tori break up (as described by KAM theory) and a chaotic sea spreads across the section, with islands of regular motion left inside it. At high energy most orbits fill whole regions of the section.
  • Flips. For equal masses and lengths released from rest, energy conservation rules out any flip over the top if 3 cos θ₁ + cos θ₂ > 2. Above that threshold flips become possible, and when they happen is extremely sensitive to the starting angles.
  • Reading the phase portraits. Each (θ, ω) panel is a two-dimensional slice of a four-dimensional flow. The background shows the vector field with the other arm frozen at its current state, and the bright curve is the true trajectory projected onto that plane.
